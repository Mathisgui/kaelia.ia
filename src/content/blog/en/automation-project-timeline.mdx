It is often the first question asked in a meeting, even before price: how long does it take to automate this or that process? The honest answer is that it depends on the scope chosen, but a well-scoped first project, limited to a single process, typically takes between two and four weeks, from the initial diagnosis to a pilot running under real conditions. Here is how that timeline actually breaks down, and what makes it move.

## Why this question comes up every time

A business owner considering automating a task rarely has time to spare: they want to know if the project settles in two weeks or drags on for months before committing. That concern is fair, because a poorly scoped project can indeed drag on, not because of the technology, but because the scope stays vague for too long or nobody validated the process's real rules.

By contrast, a project limited to a single process, with clear rules and enough volume to justify the investment, follows a fairly predictable path. Giving that predictability from the first conversation, rather than a vague estimate, is exactly what we aim for.

## Week 1: diagnosis, before any code

The first week produces no code at all. It is spent observing the process as it actually runs, not as it is described in a meeting: which tools are used, where the data lives, what the frequent exceptions are, and who needs to keep control over sensitive decisions. This is the purpose of our [free 30-minute assessment](/en/method), followed if needed by a deeper conversation with the people who run the task day to day.

This step determines whether the project is a solid candidate for automation, and sets a clear scope before any development starts. Skipping this phase to move faster is often the surest way to stretch the project out later, when an unanticipated case forces a rethink.

## Weeks 2 to 3: designing and building the pilot

Once the process is scoped, designing and building a first pilot usually takes one to two weeks. That timeline mostly depends on how many tools need connecting: a [document AI agent](/en/ai-document-agent) that has to read invoices in several formats takes longer to make reliable than an [AI reporting agent](/en/ai-reporting-agent) pulling already clean data from a spreadsheet.

The pilot is deliberately not a finished product. It covers the standard case and the most frequent exceptions, with a human validation point on anything touching money, a customer, or a decision that is hard to undo. The goal is to have something usable quickly, not something perfect on day one.

## Week 4: testing under real conditions and adjusting

The pilot is then put into real use, on a limited flow, with close monitoring. This is often when gaps show up between the process as it was described and how it actually runs on the ground. These gaps are not a failure of the initial scoping, they are a normal part of the rollout: rules get adjusted, the cases that still need a person get refined, and the volume handled automatically expands gradually.

By the end of this week, the business has an automation that genuinely runs on part of the volume, with a clear view of what still needs improving rather than a theoretical promise.

## What can push the timeline off track

Some factors clearly stretch this two-to-four-week window. The most common one is a scope that grows along the way: you start by automating invoice reminders, then want to add sales follow-ups, then the related reporting. Each addition is reasonable on its own, but stacked mid-project, it delays delivery of the first useful piece of automation. It is better to treat these extensions as follow-on projects once the first one is stable, building a chain of [automations](/en/automations) rather than a single project that never ends.

The other common factor is access to tools and data: an admin account that takes time to get created, an API with incomplete documentation, or an internal approval that takes several weeks on the client's side. These delays are not up to the provider, but they weigh just as much on the final timeline. By contrast, a project where access is ready from day one and a single person on the client side signs off on decisions moves noticeably faster.

## The takeaway

A well-scoped first automation project, limited to a single process with clear rules, typically takes between two and four weeks: one week of diagnosis, one to two weeks of designing and building the pilot, one week of testing and adjusting under real conditions. This timeline mostly stretches when the scope grows along the way or when tool access is delayed, rarely because of the technology itself.
